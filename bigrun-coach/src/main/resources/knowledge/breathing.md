# Breathing During Running

## Nose or Mouth?

During moderate and high intensity running, breathing exclusively through the nose is insufficient. The nose filters and moistens air, but its capacity is limited. Above the aerobic threshold, the body needs more oxygen than the nose can deliver — breathing through the mouth or simultaneously through both nose and mouth is natural and correct.

Nasal breathing works well during easy jogging and recovery runs. Nasal breathing training (Buteyko method) improves CO₂ tolerance and can lower resting respiratory rate, but cannot replace mouth breathing during intense effort.

## Breathing Rhythm

Rhythmic breathing synchronized with steps helps maintain consistency and reduces the risk of side stitches. Most popular patterns:

- **2:2** — inhale for 2 steps, exhale for 2 steps. Standard rhythm for marathon pace and faster. High ventilation, good for intense training.
- **3:2** — inhale for 3 steps, exhale for 2 steps. Recommended by coach Budd Coates (*Running on Air*). The asymmetric rhythm ensures landing does not always coincide with the same phase of breathing — reduces diaphragm load and risk of side stitches.
- **4:4** — inhale for 4 steps, exhale for 4 steps. For very slow running and warm-up.
- **2:1** — inhale for 2 steps, exhale for 1 step. For sprints and very intense intervals.

The 3:2 pattern is recommended by many coaches as a starting point for runners at all levels.

## Diaphragmatic (Belly) Breathing

Diaphragmatic breathing engages the lower lungs where gas exchange is most efficient. When inhaling, the belly rises — not the chest. Tense shoulders and chest block full diaphragmatic breaths. Elite runners — including Eliud Kipchoge's coaches at the NN Running group — emphasize the importance of relaxing the upper body.

How to practice: lie on your back, place a book (1-2 kg) on your belly. Inhale so the book rises and falls, not the chest. After a few weeks of conscious training, diaphragmatic breathing becomes automatic.

## Breathing and Intensity

Breathing is a natural intensity gauge:

- **Easy run** — you can speak full sentences comfortably (zone 1-2)
- **Threshold pace** — you can say 3-4 words between breaths (zone 3)
- **Intervals** — speaking is impossible, breathing heavy and fast (zone 4-5)

The talk test is a simple intensity control method without a heart rate monitor, used by USATF coaches and recommended by Jack Daniels in *Daniels' Running Formula*.

## Side Stitches and Breathing

Side stitches most often occur when starting too fast and breathing shallowly. The 3:2 pattern statistically reduces stitch frequency compared to 2:2. When a stitch occurs: slow down, take a few deep diaphragmatic breaths, gently press the painful area on exhale. Avoid eating within 2 hours before running.

## Breathing in Different Conditions

### Temperature

- **Above 25°C** — breathe through the mouth, shorter breaths. The body loses more water through breathing than in cool conditions — increase hydration. Run early morning or evening when air is cooler.
- **0°C to 10°C** — optimal zone. Nasal breathing moistens and warms air more effectively than in summer.
- **-5°C to -10°C** — breathe through the nose or cover the mouth with a buff or balaclava. The nose pre-warms air to ~30°C before it reaches the lungs.
- **Below -10°C** — balaclava or buff covering mouth and nose is mandatory. Cold, dry air inhaled directly through the mouth can irritate the bronchi and trigger exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. Reduce running pace.

### Air Quality (AQI / PM2.5)

During running, minute ventilation increases 10-20x compared to rest — you inhale significantly more pollutants than sitting at home.

- **AQI 0-50 (Good)** — run without restrictions.
- **AQI 51-100 (Moderate)** — people with asthma or respiratory sensitivity should shorten the workout.
- **AQI 101-150 (Unhealthy for sensitive groups)** — limit intensity, avoid long runs. Prefer running away from busy roads.
- **AQI above 150** — move training indoors or cancel. An FFP2 mask during running significantly impairs breathing and is impractical at high intensity.
- **PM2.5 above 75 µg/m³** — do not run outdoors. Deep breathing during intense effort at this concentration increases exposure to fine particles more than any other activity.

Check air quality before going out: IQAir, GIOS (Poland), AirVisual.

### Seaside and Coastal Areas

Sea air is humid, rich in negative ions and salt aerosol. It has a soothing effect on the airways — runners with asthma and chronic bronchial inflammation often notice improvement. High humidity (above 80%) slows sweat evaporation and increases the perceived temperature — breathing feels harder, reduce pace by 5-10% compared to normal conditions.

### Mountains and High Altitude

Above 1500 m above sea level, the partial pressure of oxygen drops — at the same breathing rate you deliver less O₂. The body compensates by increasing breathing rate and heart rate.

- **1500-2500 m** — first 3-5 days of acclimatization: reduce pace by 10-20%, breathing will be noticeably faster than at sea level.
- **Above 2500 m** — conscious deep breathing (pressure breathing technique: forceful, pressurized exhale through pursed lips) helps remove CO₂ and increase ventilation. Used by mountaineers and mountain runners.
- **Acclimatization** — after 10-14 days at altitude, red blood cell count increases and breathing stabilizes. This is the basis of altitude training (Live High, Train Low).

Mountain air is dry — risk of dehydration through breathing increases. Above 2000 m increase daily water intake by 0.5-1 liter.

## Breathing Training Outside of Running

### Swimming Pool — Most Effective Breathing Training for Runners

Swimming forces controlled, rhythmic breathing under time pressure — you cannot breathe whenever you want, only when technique allows. This builds CO₂ tolerance and strengthens respiratory muscles more effectively than most land exercises.

- **Bilateral breathing every 3 strokes** — forces an asymmetric rhythm identical to the 3:2 pattern in running. Recommended by triathlon coaches as a direct transfer to running.
- **Hypoxic pool training** — breathing every 5, 7, or 9 strokes. Raises CO₂ tolerance, increases the efficiency of each breath. Used by Kenyan and Ethiopian runners as a supplement to altitude training.
- **Kickboard drill** — hold a kickboard, work only with legs, focus exclusively on breathing rhythm without thinking about arm technique. Excellent for beginners.

2-3 swimming sessions per week for 6-8 weeks measurably improve breathing economy during running.

### Land Breathing Exercises

**Box Breathing**
Technique used by Navy SEALs and endurance athletes to control the nervous system:
- Inhale for 4 seconds
- Hold for 4 seconds
- Exhale for 4 seconds
- Hold for 4 seconds

5-10 minutes daily lowers resting respiratory rate and improves breath control under effort.

**Diaphragmatic Breathing with Load**
Lie on your back, place a book (1-2 kg) on your belly. Inhale so the book rises and falls — not the chest. 3 sets of 20 breaths. After 4 weeks, runners report a noticeable reduction in respiratory fatigue on long runs.

**Pursed Lip Breathing**
Inhale through nose for 2 seconds, exhale through slightly pursed lips for 4-6 seconds. Increases pressure in the airways, improves gas exchange in the alveoli. Recommended by sports pulmonologists for runners with a tendency toward shallow breathing.

**Inspiratory Muscle Training (Powerbreathe, Airofit)**
Devices with adjustable inspiratory resistance strengthen the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. Research in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* (McConnell, 2011) shows a 15% improvement in time to exhaustion after 6 weeks of training. Used by professionals — including preparations for the Tokyo Olympics by GB Athletics representatives.

**Yoga and Pranayama**
- **Nadi Shodhana** (alternate nostril breathing) — regulates the nervous system, improves breath awareness
- **Kapalabhati** (rapid, energetic exhales) — strengthens the diaphragm, clears the airways
- **Ujjayi** (throat breathing with sound) — used by ultramarathon runners to control intensity over long distances

20 minutes of breathing yoga 3x per week for 8 weeks lowered breathing rate during submaximal running by an average of 2-3 breaths per minute (University of Exeter, 2018).
