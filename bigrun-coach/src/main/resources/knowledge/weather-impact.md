# Weather Impact on Running Performance

## Temperature and Running Performance

Temperature is one of the most significant external factors affecting running performance. The optimal temperature range for distance running is **8-12°C** — most marathon world records have been set in this range.

Research from the *Journal of Applied Physiology* shows that for every 1°C above 15°C, marathon finishing time increases by approximately 0.4% for recreational runners and 0.3% for elite athletes. At 25°C, a runner capable of a 3-hour marathon in ideal conditions may expect to finish in 3:10-3:15.

**Temperature zones and their impact:**

| Temperature | Impact | Recommended adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Below 0°C | Increased energy expenditure, airway risk | Reduce pace 5-8%, layer clothing |
| 0-8°C | Slightly suboptimal, manageable | Reduce pace 2-3% |
| 8-12°C | Optimal for racing | No adjustment needed |
| 12-18°C | Good conditions | No adjustment needed |
| 18-24°C | Moderate heat stress | Reduce pace 5-8%, increase hydration |
| 24-28°C | Significant heat stress | Reduce pace 10-15%, shorten workout |
| Above 28°C | Dangerous for long runs | Avoid racing, run early morning only |

## Wind and Running Performance

Wind has a disproportionate effect on running economy. Headwind increases energy cost significantly, while tailwind provides a smaller benefit.

- **Headwind at 10 km/h** — increases energy cost by approximately 3%
- **Headwind at 20 km/h** — increases energy cost by approximately 10%
- **Headwind at 30 km/h** — increases energy cost by approximately 20%
- **Tailwind** — provides roughly 50% of the energy savings of an equivalent headwind

The **wind chill factor** at low temperatures dramatically increases perceived cold. At -5°C with 30 km/h wind, the felt temperature drops to approximately -15°C — significantly increasing frostbite and hypothermia risk.

**Tactical approach to wind in races:** run into the headwind in the first half, use tailwind in the second half. Drafting behind another runner reduces wind resistance by 20-40%.

## Humidity

Humidity affects the body's ability to cool itself through sweat evaporation. At high humidity, sweat does not evaporate efficiently, causing core temperature to rise faster.

- **Below 40%** — good evaporation, efficient cooling
- **40-60%** — moderate, manageable
- **60-80%** — cooling efficiency drops, perceived effort increases
- **Above 80%** — high heat stress even at moderate temperatures. A combination of 25°C and 90% humidity is physiologically more demanding than 32°C at 30% humidity.

The **Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT)** index combines temperature, humidity, wind, and solar radiation into a single heat stress score. Used by World Athletics to assess race-day conditions:
- WBGT below 17°C — low risk
- WBGT 17-22°C — moderate risk
- WBGT above 28°C — high risk, races may be cancelled

## Rain

Light rain in warm conditions is beneficial — it helps cool the body and reduces heat stress. Problems arise in specific combinations:

- **Cold rain below 10°C** — high hypothermia risk on long runs. Wet clothing loses insulation properties. Wear a water-resistant outer layer.
- **Rain above 20°C** — no significant negative impact, often welcome cooling
- **Heavy rain** — reduces visibility, increases injury risk on technical terrain, soaked shoes increase blister risk

## Solar Radiation

Running in direct sunlight adds 4-8°C to the perceived temperature compared to running in shade at the same air temperature. This effect is strongest between 10:00 and 16:00. Apply SPF 30+ sunscreen for runs exceeding 45 minutes outdoors, especially above 1500 m altitude where UV intensity increases significantly.

## Air Quality and Running

During running, minute ventilation increases 10-20x compared to rest — you inhale significantly more pollutants than sitting at home.

- **AQI 0-50 (Good)** — run without restrictions
- **AQI 51-100 (Moderate)** — people with asthma or respiratory sensitivity should shorten the workout
- **AQI 101-150 (Unhealthy for sensitive groups)** — limit intensity, avoid long runs, prefer routes away from busy roads
- **AQI above 150** — move training indoors or cancel
- **PM2.5 above 75 µg/m³** — do not run outdoors

Check air quality before going out: IQAir, GIOS (Poland), AirVisual.

## Running in Specific Weather Environments

### Mountains and High Altitude

Above 1500 m above sea level, the partial pressure of oxygen drops. The body compensates by increasing breathing rate and heart rate. Reduce pace by 10-20% during the first 3-5 days of acclimatization. After 10-14 days, red blood cell count increases and performance stabilizes — the basis of Live High, Train Low altitude training.

Mountain air is dry — increase daily water intake by 0.5-1 liter above 2000 m. UV radiation is stronger — sunscreen and UV-protective sunglasses are essential.

### Seaside and Coastal Running

Sea air is humid and rich in negative ions and salt aerosol — soothing for the airways. High humidity above 80% slows sweat evaporation — reduce pace by 5-10% compared to normal conditions. Coastal wind can be strong and unpredictable; plan routes to run into headwind on the way out and tailwind on the way back.

### Hot and Dry Climates (Desert Conditions)

Dry heat allows efficient sweat evaporation — the body cools effectively but dehydration risk is high. Sweat losses can exceed 2 liters per hour. Carry water on any run exceeding 45 minutes. Run before 8:00 or after 18:00. Watch for signs of heat exhaustion: dizziness, stopping sweating, confusion — stop immediately and cool down.

### Cold and Snowy Conditions

Snow and ice dramatically change running biomechanics — shorter stride, lower foot lift, more cautious foot placement. Expect pace to be 20-30% slower on packed snow compared to dry road. Use trail shoes with aggressive grip or screw-in traction devices on ice. Dress in moisture-wicking base layers — cotton retains moisture and accelerates hypothermia.

## Extreme Conditions — Decision Guide

| Condition | Recommended Action |
|---|---|
| Temperature above 35°C | Cancel or move indoors |
| Temperature below -15°C | Move indoors or significantly shorten |
| Wind above 50 km/h | Cancel — injury risk on technical surfaces |
| Thunderstorm | Stop immediately, seek shelter |
| AQI above 150 | Move indoors |
| Ice on surface | Traction devices or treadmill |
| WBGT above 28°C | Cancel racing, easy running only |

## Heat Acclimatization

The body adapts to heat training within **10-14 days** of consistent exposure. Plasma volume increases by 5-10%, sweat onset occurs earlier and at lower core temperature.

Practical heat acclimatization protocol used by elite marathon runners before hot-weather races (Tokyo, Chicago):
- 30-45 minutes of easy running in hot conditions daily for 10-14 days
- Or post-run sauna sessions (20 minutes at 80°C) — research from University of Oregon shows equivalent adaptations to outdoor heat exposure

## Cold Acclimatization

After 2-3 weeks of regular cold exposure, the body improves peripheral circulation and reduces shivering threshold. Experienced winter runners tolerate temperatures that would sideline unacclimatized runners. Start cold weather running with shorter sessions and gradually increase duration as the body adapts.
