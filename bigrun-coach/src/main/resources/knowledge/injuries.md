# Running Injuries — Prevention and Management

## Most Common Running Injuries

### Runner's Knee (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome)

The most frequently reported running injury, accounting for approximately 25% of all running-related complaints. Pain around or behind the kneecap, worsening when running downhill, descending stairs, or sitting for prolonged periods.

**Causes:** weak hip abductors and gluteal muscles allowing the knee to cave inward (valgus collapse), overtraining, sudden mileage increases, worn-out shoes, and excessive downhill running.

**Prevention:**
- Single-leg squats and clamshells to strengthen hip abductors
- Avoid increasing weekly mileage by more than 10%
- Replace running shoes every 600-800 km
- Reduce downhill running during high-mileage phases

**Management:** reduce mileage by 50%, avoid downhill running, apply ice 15-20 minutes after runs, strengthen hip and gluteal muscles. Most cases resolve within 4-8 weeks with appropriate load management.

### Iliotibial Band Syndrome (IT Band Syndrome)

Sharp or burning pain on the outer knee, typically appearing after a consistent distance (e.g., always at km 8). Caused by the IT band rubbing against the lateral femoral condyle.

**Causes:** weak hip abductors, excessive hip adduction during running, sudden mileage increases, cambered road surfaces, leg length discrepancy.

**Prevention:** hip strengthening (lateral band walks, side-lying leg raises), avoid always running on the same side of a cambered road, gradual mileage increases.

**Management:** temporary reduction in mileage, foam rolling the IT band and gluteus medius (not the painful spot itself), hip strengthening. Foam rolling at the lateral knee is controversial — some physiotherapists advise against it. Steroid injections are effective for acute cases. Recovery: 4-12 weeks.

### Plantar Fasciitis

Pain on the bottom of the heel, worst with the first steps in the morning or after prolonged sitting. The plantar fascia — a thick band of tissue connecting the heel to the toes — becomes inflamed or develops micro-tears.

**Causes:** sudden mileage increases, tight calf muscles and Achilles tendon, low or high arches, transitioning too quickly to minimalist footwear, hard surfaces.

**Prevention:**
- Daily calf stretching and eccentric calf raises
- Gradual transition between shoe types
- Avoid barefoot walking on hard floors when symptomatic

**Management:** eccentric single-leg heel drops on a step (the Alfredson protocol — 3 sets of 15 reps twice daily), frozen water bottle rolling under the foot, night splints for severe cases. Recovery: 3-6 months for chronic cases. Running through mild plantar fasciitis is possible with load management; severe cases require rest.

### Achilles Tendinopathy

Pain and stiffness in the Achilles tendon, typically 2-6 cm above the heel insertion. Morning stiffness that improves with warmup is characteristic. Distinct from Achilles tendon rupture — a complete rupture produces sudden severe pain and inability to push off.

**Causes:** sudden speed or mileage increases, excessive uphill running, inadequate recovery between hard sessions, tight calf muscles, transitioning to lower heel-drop shoes too quickly.

**Prevention:**
- Eccentric calf raises (slow lowering phase) performed consistently
- Gradual heel-drop reduction when switching shoes
- Adequate recovery between hard training sessions

**Management:** eccentric calf protocol (Alfredson or Silbernagel protocol), reduce mileage but do not completely stop — tendon responds better to load than to rest. Avoid stretching in acute phase. NSAIDs are not recommended for tendinopathy — they may impair tendon healing. Recovery: 6-12 weeks for reactive tendinopathy, up to 6 months for degenerative cases.

### Shin Splints (Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome)

Diffuse pain along the inner border of the tibia, covering a broad area (distinguishes it from a stress fracture, which produces sharp point tenderness). Common in new runners and those returning after a break.

**Causes:** too much, too fast, too soon. Sudden mileage increase, hard surfaces, inadequate footwear, weak hip and ankle stabilizers.

**Prevention:** gradual mileage increases, mix of running surfaces, hip and ankle strengthening, adequate recovery.

**Management:** reduce mileage by 50%, run on softer surfaces, strengthen hip abductors and ankle dorsiflexors. If pain persists beyond 4 weeks or becomes point-specific, get imaging to rule out a tibial stress fracture. Recovery: 2-6 weeks.

### Stress Fractures

Stress fractures are overuse injuries resulting from repetitive bone loading without adequate recovery. Most common sites in runners: tibia, metatarsals, navicular, and femoral neck. Point tenderness over bone (not muscle) and pain that worsens progressively during a run are warning signs.

**Risk factors:** sudden mileage increases, low bone density, inadequate calcium and vitamin D intake, low energy availability (RED-S), hard surfaces, worn-out shoes.

**Management:** immediate cessation of running. Diagnosis requires MRI (X-rays miss early stress fractures). Recovery: 6-12 weeks of non-impact activity. High-risk locations (femoral neck, navicular, anterior tibial cortex) may require surgical intervention and carry longer recovery timelines.

**Warning:** running through a stress fracture risks complete fracture — seek medical evaluation promptly if bone pain is suspected.

## Injury Prevention Principles

### The 10% Rule

Do not increase weekly mileage by more than 10% from one week to the next. The most consistently cited injury prevention guideline in running medicine. Similarly, do not increase both mileage and intensity simultaneously.

### Training Load Management

80% of weekly running should be at easy effort (conversational pace, zone 1-2). Only 20% at moderate to high intensity. This polarized approach, used by elite coaches including Renato Canova and Stellan Skarsgard, maximizes adaptation while minimizing injury risk.

**Hard-easy principle:** never schedule two hard sessions on consecutive days. Recovery between quality sessions is when adaptation occurs.

### Strength Training for Injury Prevention

Runners who include strength training have significantly lower injury rates. Key areas:

**Gluteal complex:** single-leg squats, Romanian deadlifts, hip thrusts, clamshells. Weak glutes are implicated in runner's knee, IT band syndrome, and hamstring injuries.

**Calf and Achilles:** single-leg calf raises with slow eccentric phase, jump rope, plyometrics. The Achilles tendon stores and returns elastic energy — it needs progressive loading to remain resilient.

**Hip flexors:** lunges, step-ups, hip flexor strengthening. Weak hip flexors reduce running economy and contribute to lower back pain during long runs.

**Foot and ankle:** single-leg balance, toe spreads, towel scrunches. Foot strength is underemphasized — research shows foot strengthening programs reduce plantar fasciitis and metatarsal stress fracture risk.

### Footwear and Surfaces

Running shoe lifespan: **600-800 km**. Midsole compression degrades before the outer sole shows visible wear — many runners continue in dead shoes without realizing it.

Surface variety reduces repetitive stress:
- Asphalt and concrete — highest impact, highest speed
- Trail and grass — lower impact, engages stabilizing muscles
- Treadmill — consistent surface, useful in bad weather
- Track — soft surface, good for speed work

Vary surfaces regularly. Avoid always running on the same side of a cambered road.

## When to See a Doctor

Seek medical evaluation for:
- Pain that does not improve with 2 weeks of reduced training
- Point tenderness over bone
- Swelling that does not resolve within 48 hours
- Pain that causes limping during or after running
- Any sudden severe pain during a run

Self-diagnosing and continuing to train through significant pain is the most common reason running injuries become chronic. Early intervention typically means faster return to running.
