# Hydration for Runners

## Why Hydration Matters

Water makes up approximately 60% of body weight and is critical for virtually every physiological process relevant to running: oxygen transport, muscle contraction, temperature regulation, and nutrient delivery. Even mild dehydration of **1-2% of body weight** measurably impairs endurance performance.

Research from the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* shows:
- 1% dehydration — core temperature rises faster, perceived effort increases
- 2% dehydration — aerobic capacity drops by 4-6%
- 3% dehydration — risk of heat exhaustion increases significantly
- 5% dehydration — performance declines by up to 30%, cognitive function impaired

## How Much to Drink

**General daily baseline:** 35-45 ml per kg of body weight. A 70 kg runner needs approximately 2.5-3.0 liters per day at rest, more on training days.

**During running:**
- Easy runs under 45 minutes in cool conditions — no drinking necessary if well-hydrated beforehand
- Runs of 45-75 minutes — 400-600 ml per hour
- Runs over 75 minutes — 500-800 ml per hour, with electrolytes
- Hot and humid conditions — increase by 200-400 ml per hour

**Sweat rate calculation:** weigh yourself before and after a 60-minute run without drinking. Every 1 kg of weight lost equals approximately 1 liter of fluid lost. This gives your personal sweat rate — use it to calibrate intake for race day.

Average sweat rates range from 0.5 to 2.5 liters per hour depending on intensity, temperature, and individual physiology. Elite marathon runners competing in heat can lose up to 3 liters per hour.

## Electrolytes

Sweat contains not just water but electrolytes — primarily sodium, chloride, potassium, and magnesium. Replacing only water without electrolytes during prolonged exercise leads to hyponatremia (dangerously low blood sodium).

**Hyponatremia** is more common than dehydration as a cause of collapse in recreational marathoners. It occurs when runners drink excessive amounts of plain water, diluting blood sodium below 135 mmol/L. Symptoms: nausea, headache, swelling of hands and feet, confusion, seizures. Fatal cases have been recorded at major marathons.

**Sodium needs during running:**
- Under 60 minutes — electrolytes not needed if diet is adequate
- 60-90 minutes — 300-500 mg sodium per hour recommended
- Over 90 minutes — 500-700 mg sodium per hour, more in heat

**Electrolyte sources:** sports drinks (typically 400-700 mg sodium per liter), electrolyte tablets (SaltStick, Precision Hydration), salted gels, or simply salted food.

**Salty sweaters:** some runners lose significantly more sodium than average. Signs: white residue on skin or clothing after running, frequent muscle cramps, excessive thirst relative to sweat volume. These runners need higher sodium intake — up to 1000-1500 mg per hour in hot conditions.

## Pre-Run Hydration

**2-3 hours before:** drink 500-600 ml of water or sports drink. This allows time for excess fluid to be excreted.

**15-30 minutes before:** drink an additional 150-250 ml. Avoid excessive drinking immediately before running — full stomach impairs performance.

**Urine color check:** the simplest hydration indicator. Pale yellow (like lemonade) = well hydrated. Dark yellow (like apple juice) = dehydrated. Clear = possibly overhydrated. Use this as a daily habit, especially on race morning.

## Hydration During a Marathon

World Athletics and IAAF recommend drinking to thirst during marathon running rather than following a fixed schedule. Drinking to thirst prevents both dehydration and overhydration.

**Practical race strategy:**
- Take water at every aid station in hot conditions, every other station in cool conditions
- Small sips (100-150 ml) are more effective than large volumes at once
- Alternate water and sports drink at aid stations for combined hydration and carbohydrate delivery
- Do not try new sports drinks on race day — train with your race-day nutrition

**Drinking while running:** squeeze the top of the cup to form a funnel — reduces spillage. Slow slightly at aid stations rather than grabbing cups at full speed.

## Post-Run Rehydration

Rehydration after running requires more than just replacing lost fluid volume because the kidneys continue to excrete fluid even when dehydrated.

**Formula:** drink 1.25-1.5 liters of fluid for every 1 kg of body weight lost during the run. Include sodium in recovery drinks — it stimulates thirst and helps retain fluid.

**Recovery drinks:** chocolate milk has been validated by multiple studies as an effective recovery drink — it provides carbohydrates, protein, sodium, and fluid in an optimal ratio. Commercial recovery drinks with 3:1 or 4:1 carbohydrate-to-protein ratio also work well.

Avoid alcohol within 2 hours post-run — it is a diuretic and impairs glycogen resynthesis and muscle repair.

## Hydration in Different Conditions

**Hot weather (above 25°C):**
Pre-cool strategies before long runs — cold towels, ice vests, cold water immersion of forearms. Carry more fluid than you think you need. Consider a hydration vest for runs over 90 minutes.

**Cold weather (below 5°C):**
Thirst sensation is reduced in cold — you may not feel thirsty despite significant fluid losses. Drink on a schedule, not only when thirsty. Cold air is dry — respiratory fluid losses increase substantially in winter.

**High altitude (above 2000 m):**
Increased respiratory rate and dry mountain air dramatically increase fluid loss through breathing. Add 0.5-1 liter to daily intake. Altitude also suppresses appetite — active effort needed to maintain nutrition and hydration.

**Long trail runs and ultras:**
Fluid needs vary dramatically with terrain, elevation, and temperature. Learn to drink while moving on technical terrain. Carry a minimum of 500 ml per hour with resupply points planned.

## Caffeine and Hydration

Caffeine is a mild diuretic at high doses but studies show that habitual coffee drinkers do not experience meaningful dehydration from moderate caffeine intake (up to 400 mg per day). Pre-race coffee (200-300 mg caffeine, 60 minutes before) improves performance by 2-4% and does not require additional hydration compensation in acclimatized individuals.

## Signs of Dehydration During Running

Stop or significantly slow down if you experience: dizziness, headache, cessation of sweating despite heat, dark urine, muscle cramps unrelated to effort, confusion, or heart rate significantly higher than expected for the pace. Move to shade, drink electrolyte solution, and cool down before continuing.
